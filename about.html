How the models work

What the 3D models are made of, and what physics the simulated showers contain. Both are simplified on purpose: the goal is to show how an imaging Cherenkov array works, not to replace a full simulation.

The principle

A gamma ray hits the upper atmosphere and starts a cascade of electrons, positrons and photons. These particles move faster than light does in air, so they emit Cherenkov light: a blue flash a few nanoseconds long. The light falls on the ground in a disc, the light pool, about 120 m in radius. A telescope inside the pool focuses the flash on a fast camera and records an image of the shower. The shape and orientation of the image give the direction and energy of the gamma ray. Several telescopes seeing the same shower from different places give a stereoscopic view, which sharpens the reconstruction.

The 3D models

Everything is drawn with three.js (r128), straight from code. There are no imported meshes, no textures from photos and no build step. Open the HTML file and it runs.

  • Telescopes. Each type has a builder in js/ (lst.js, mst.js, sst.js, plus magic.js). It assembles the dish, the facets, the camera support and the mount from primitives: paraboloids, hexagonal facets, tubes, boxes. The LST has 198 facets, the MST a Davies-Cotton dish, the SST dual-mirror optics.
  • Cameras. The LST and MST cameras have 1855 pixels. Their positions come from the prod6 camera description (js/lstcam.js). The SST camera has 2048 SiPM pixels.
  • Arrays. js/array.js places the telescopes at their prod6 positions (CTAO-North) or from the prod5 and prod6 layouts (CTAO-South), points them all at the same direction, and adds the terrain, the night sky and the landmarks.
  • Accuracy. Dimensions and positions come from published values where they exist. Details such as the tower, the drives or the counterweights are estimated from photos. The terrain, roads and vegetation are illustrative. Each telescope has a summary file in the repository that tags every value as sourced or estimated.

The shower model

The array pages simulate gamma-ray showers with closed-form formulas. The model reproduces the right trends with energy. It is not CORSIKA or sim_telarray. The full list of values and their sources is in SHOWER_MODEL.md.

1. Energy

By default, the energy is drawn uniformly in log E, so that every energy range shows up: 15 GeV to 100 TeV at La Palma, 50 GeV to 300 TeV at Paranal. The "realistic" option draws from a Crab-like power law, dN/dE ∝ E−2.5, where only about 1 event in 1000 is above 1 TeV.

2. Longitudinal development

The depth of the shower maximum follows Xmax = X0 ln(E/Ec), with X0 = 36.7 g/cm² and Ec = 83 MeV. Higher energy means a deeper shower, about 85 g/cm² more per decade. Each shower gets a random shift of about 35 g/cm². The number of particles along the axis follows the Greisen profile. Cherenkov emission per g/cm² does not depend on altitude, so this profile weights the emission points directly. Depth is converted to altitude with an exponential atmosphere, X(h) = 1030 g/cm² · exp(−h/7.6 km).

3. Light on the ground

Inside the pool the model gives about 90 photons/m² per TeV (300–550 nm) at about 2000 m. The pool radius is about 120 m, set by the Cherenkov angle and the height of emission. The lateral profile is flat inside the ring with a hump at 120 m. It falls off outside the ring over a length that grows slowly with energy. Above 1 TeV a core peak appears, because deeper showers fill in the centre. The light pool drawn on the ground uses this same profile, so it grows and brightens with energy.

4. In each telescope

  • Photoelectrons. Photon density × effective mirror area × 10%. The 10% covers the atmosphere, the mirrors and the photodetectors. Mirror areas: LST 369 m², MST 88 m², SST about 6 m².
  • Image. Emission points along the axis, weighted by the Greisen profile, are projected into each camera. Each point is spread by about 10 m across the axis, which gives an image width of about 0.1° at 1 TeV. The image length and its distance from the source position grow with the impact distance.
  • Pixel colours. Log scale, from faint blue at about 5 photoelectrons to saturated yellow at about 300.
  • Trigger. At least 50 photoelectrons in the camera, with at least 3 pixels above 5. This gives thresholds of about 20 GeV for the LST, 100 GeV for the MST and 1 TeV for the SST.
  • Timing. Arrival time = path length from each emission point plus the refractive delay of the air. Near the axis the end of the image nearest the source position arrives last. Beyond about 100 m the order flips. MAGIC has observed this effect. The event display slows time to about 0.12 s per nanosecond inside an image, and compresses delays between telescopes to at most about 1 s.

At high energy the real limit is often the camera field of view, not the photon count. The model handles this: light that lands outside the camera is lost.

What is not modelled

  • Hadronic background showers (cosmic rays), which dominate real data.
  • Night-sky background noise in the pixels.
  • The angular distribution of the electrons beyond the 10 m lateral spread.
  • The geomagnetic field.
  • Differences in atmospheric absorption between sites.
  • The exact camera trigger logic.
Treat the numbers as illustrations. Some values are cited from the literature, some are derived from first principles, and a few are heuristics tuned to match published anchors. SHOWER_MODEL.md tags each one.
